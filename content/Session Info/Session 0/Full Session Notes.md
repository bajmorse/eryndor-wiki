# Full Session Notes

Expanded background on Sky-Bough Village and the troubles facing it, developed during Session 0.

## Sky-Bough Village

The session began high among the colossal trees of an ancient forest in southwestern Ilyndor, between the Windrider Cliffs, the Frostfang Mountains, and the Summering Sea, in the picturesque Sky-Bough Village.

By the standards of Ilyndor, Sky-Bough is a substantial settlement. By the standards of the civilized kingdoms across the sea, it is little more than an isolated woodland village.

Wooden homes, workshops, shrines, platforms, and gardens cling to enormous trunks and branches hundreds of feet above the forest floor. Rope bridges connect different neighbourhoods, while lifts, cranes, ladders, baskets, and pulley systems move people and goods vertically through the settlement.

Sky-Bough is predominantly Kenku, with a significant Simiah population, a smaller community of Katari, and scattered Humans, Infernis, and other peoples. It has traditionally been a strange but comfortable place: isolated enough to maintain its traditions, but close enough to the Summering Sea and several overland routes to see merchants and travellers.

## The Party

Amongst the hustle and bustle on the skywalks of Sky-Bough Village, a group of five quietly gathered in a hanging tavern as the sun set through the lush canopy above.

[[Aangry Mank]], a grizzled male Simiah with a mane and beard of white fur, sat with his quiver and a pair of well-worn short swords close at hand. Glowing blue tattoos coiled across his bald scalp and forearm, and strings of feathers and beads hung from his wrists and belt. Sky-Bough was his home, and he was rarely found in it without [[Buffa-lo Bill]], his loyal albino buffalo, somewhere nearby.

Beside him sat [[Finnegan Endercast]], a male Infernis war wizard with violet skin and a pair of long, gold-banded horns curling back from his brow. Dark hair fell loosely around a sharp, angular face, and a spellbook and scrollcase hung from his belt beneath deep red and black robes. Like Aangry, Finnegan had lived in Sky-Bough long enough that it had become home in truth, if not by birth.

Across from them sat [[Frayne Castel]], a broad-shouldered human man with a close-cropped, greying beard and a face marked by old scars. He wore dark leather and chainmail beneath a fur-lined collar, a stark white skull painted across his chest, and kept a hand crossbow and long dagger within easy reach even at rest. Frayne had come to Sky-Bough from Goldhorn Crossing, a nearby settlement destroyed years before in a territorial conflict that had taken his family with it — years of wandering had followed before Sky-Bough became the place he finally stopped running.

Beside him, [[Nyx Ashwisk]] sat curled comfortably into her chair. A female Katari hedge witch, her black fur was streaked with deep red, matching the tiger-stripe markings across her face, and a wide-brimmed witch's hat sat atop her head. Satchels of vials, dried flowers, and other apothecary's tools hung at her hip, and her familiar, [[Snowberry]], a small white ferret, was very likely tucked away somewhere close by. Nyx traveled with a nomadic Katari band circling the Heartwood, and her work as an apothecary carried her through communities all across the region — but Sky-Bough had become one of her favourite stops, partly for the company, and substantially for the Skybound Pale Ale.

Last to the table was [[Zella Ironstone]], a tall, pale blue-skinned woman, daughter of an Infernis and an Ice Giant, with a pair of curling horns and long red hair worn in braids. Faint scars and old bloodstains marked her fur-trimmed leather armor, and a pair of wolf-hilted swords hung at her waist. Born in the Frostfang Mountains, Zella wandered Ilyndor extensively, and returned to Sky-Bough whenever her travels brought her close by.

None of the five were strangers to one another, nor to the table they now shared. They were not adventurers brought together by fate, but old friends and regular drinking companions, gathering as they often did whenever their travels allowed it.

Over pints of Skybound Pale Ale, the five caught each other up on the gossip, news, and goings-on they'd each heard since they were last together, months apart. What follows is everything that came up as they talked.

## The Bent Feather

At the social centre of Sky-Bough sits the Bent Feather Tavern, home of the famous Skybound Pale Ale. The tavern is run by Joe, its longtime barkeep and brewmaster. Joe is one of those people who seems to know everyone, hears everything, and remembers which drink somebody wants before they order it.

More importantly, Joe brews Skybound Pale Ale — the village's most famous export, produced nowhere else in Ilyndor. And there is a reason for that.

### The Secret of Skybound Pale Ale

One ingredient cannot be exported: water from the Sacred Spring. Joe discovered years ago that using a small amount of spring water during brewing produces the ale's distinctive character. The village mayor controls its commercial distribution, while Joe controls its production, and that arrangement has worked remarkably well.

Until now.

The Sacred Spring has begun drying up, so Joe can't brew nearly as much Skybound as he once could. Meanwhile, the mayor has been cultivating relationships with merchants, travellers, and political contacts from beyond Ilyndor, and has quietly prioritized some of the dwindling supply for them. From her perspective this is sensible — maintaining those relationships may be increasingly important to Sky-Bough. To the villagers, it looks like there's no ale for them anymore, but the mayor's foreign friends never seem thirsty.

Because Joe physically makes the ale, much of the anger has landed on him. That's the root of the rumor that he's somehow trying to "maintain control over the town." He isn't — but people increasingly think he is.

## The Sky-Bough Martial Arts Tournament

At the end of every month, Sky-Bough hosts its martial arts tournament — traditionally one of the village's biggest social occasions, drawing fighters and spectators alike out onto the platforms and clearings set aside for it. Joe hosts the tournament as well as the tavern, but the two are entirely separate affairs: one is where the village drinks, the other is where it settles who speaks for it.

The prize isn't gold or goods, but standing. Whoever wins is granted the honour of representing Sky-Bough for the following month, whenever a dignitary is needed to treat with surrounding villages — gatherings in the Heartwood chief among them.

Lately, though, interest has been falling. Fewer spectators turn out each month, and fewer fighters still are willing to step into the ring. Some mutter that the shrinking field is no accident — that the tournament is really just Joe's way of controlling who gets to represent the town, and that fewer people bother entering because they suspect the outcome is already decided.

## The Mayor

The mayor is a young Kenku woman — energetic, ambitious, charismatic, and particularly popular among Sky-Bough's younger inhabitants. She represents a generation that sees Sky-Bough becoming something more than an isolated village hidden in the trees: trade is increasing, outsiders are coming, and Skybound Pale Ale is becoming known beyond Ilyndor. She wants to capitalize on that.

And therein lies the problem. Older residents increasingly see her as selling pieces of Sky-Bough to outsiders, and the shortage has made that resentment much worse. She isn't necessarily corrupt — if anything, she genuinely believes she is securing Sky-Bough's future. She's just doing it at possibly the worst imaginable moment.

## The Parkers

The Parkers, a Katari family of bakers, are beloved throughout Sky-Bough. Their reputation rests on one seemingly impossible fact: they always have pastries. Festivals, funerals, birthdays, random Tuesday afternoons — pastries. There's a widely repeated story that the Parkers possess some ancient secret family recipe. They don't; they're perfectly normal pastries. The Parkers are simply extremely generous people.

But recently, even they are running short. Flour, fruit, nuts, grain, and other staples coming from farms surrounding Sky-Bough have become increasingly difficult to obtain — and that might be the first visible consequence of something considerably darker.

## The Ancient Pact

Long before Sky-Bough became what it is today, its people made a pact. Nobody agrees anymore about who — or what — the pact was made with, but everyone knows its terms: once each year, before the growing season, one resident of Sky-Bough is selected by lot, and that person is sacrificed. In exchange, the forests remain relatively safe, dangerous creatures keep their distance, and the surrounding lands produce an unusually reliable harvest.

During the ritual, Sky-Bough closes. Nobody enters, nobody leaves. Travellers familiar with the region know not to approach — those who ignored the warnings historically had an uncomfortable tendency to disappear. The practice is horrifying, but it has worked for generations.

Until last year, when the sacrifice escaped. The selected victim fled before the ritual could be completed, and nobody knows where they went. For the first time in living memory, the pact was left unfulfilled.

Months later, the consequences are beginning to appear: crop yields are down, wildlife is behaving strangely, predators are appearing closer to settled areas, the Sacred Spring is receding, and the Parkers can't acquire enough ingredients. People are beginning to ask the question nobody wants answered — was the sacrifice actually necessary?

## The Sacred Spring

Deep within the forest below Sky-Bough lies an ancient spring. Its waters supposedly slow the effects of aging — the stories are exaggerated, but not entirely false. Drinking from it occasionally seems to improve vitality, accelerate recovery, and subtly slow aging. Continuous consumption, however, has consequences.

And that brings us to one of Sky-Bough's darkest stories.

### The Drowning

Decades ago, a religious community became obsessed with the spring. They believed its ability to preserve life proved something profound: that death itself was a test. The faithful believed those who willingly surrendered their lives in the sacred waters would transcend mortality rather than merely delay it.

One night, dozens entered the spring. None returned. Their bodies were recovered over the following days, but according to those present, something impossible happened afterward — for several nights, lights moved beneath the water.

The incident eventually faded into local folklore, and centuries of retelling have reduced what happened to "that time a cult drowned themselves in the spring." Except now the spring is drying, and objects that haven't seen daylight since the drowning are beginning to emerge from beneath the waterline — including symbols, some of which bear an uncomfortable resemblance to the symbols being found where children disappear.

## The Testers and Sir Testalot

The Testers, followers of Sir Testalot, are a small but rapidly growing religious movement. Their theology holds that mortal existence consists of a series of divine tests — hardship is therefore not punishment, but an opportunity deliberately placed before someone by the divine. Most Testers are harmless, and some are genuinely wonderful people, but the theology occasionally produces absolute lunatics.

Recently, something resembling a gigantic moustache appeared in the clouds above Sky-Bough. The Testers interpreted it as a divine sign pointing toward the Summering Sea, and since then devotees have been growing moustaches, watching the skies, and arguing over its meaning. Two interpretations dominate: that the Sea People have violated the ancient pact, or that the escaped sacrifice fled toward the sea and must be recovered.

Scholars examining surviving writings from the old Drowning cult have found that it followed an ancient faith whose teachings contain phrases eerily similar to modern Tester doctrine. The Testers are not the same religion as that old cult, but the resemblance raises an uncomfortable possibility: Sir Testalot may be a ridiculous modern interpretation of something much older.

## The Sea People

"Sea People" isn't an actual name — it's simply what frightened inland villagers call them. They're a maritime culture living along the Summering Sea, primarily Sea Elves alongside other coastal peoples, and for generations their relationship with inland Ilyndor has been distant but peaceful.

Recently, coastal fishing settlements have been attacked, and survivors insist the attackers came from the sea. The simple version making the rounds inland is "the Sea People are attacking us" — but that isn't quite true. Something has changed beneath the Summering Sea: fishing grounds have become unreliable, strange creatures are appearing, and some coastal communities have been abandoned. Armed bands have begun raiding inland settlements, but they aren't an organized invasion. Some are refugees, some are raiders, and some are looking for something — possibly even the escaped sacrifice, though why remains unknown.

## Old Man Willy

Old Man Willy is an elderly Simiah who has spent decades collecting strange objects from throughout the forest — old coins, broken idols, bits of pottery, weapons, bones, carved stones, and fragments of things nobody recognizes. Naturally, everyone has decided he's a necromancer.

Unfortunately, Willy has recently started appearing at the Bent Feather every evening, despite having avoided the tavern for years, and everyone assumes he's up to something. He's recognized the symbols appearing where the children disappear, because he owns something bearing the same symbol, and he knows where he found it. He has been coming to the tavern because he wants to tell somebody — he just hasn't decided who he trusts.

## The Forgotten Hero and the Sealed Tomb

Generations ago, something emerged from beneath the forest. Stories call it a demon. A travelling sorcerer confronted it but couldn't destroy it, and instead sealed the creature underground, promising: "Someday, I will return and finish what I began." They never returned.

Eventually history became folklore, folklore became myth, and myth became something parents told children — until farmers clearing new land uncovered the entrance to the tomb. The seals are real, the inscriptions are real, and something is definitely inside. Naturally, the local priests have reached the only reasonable conclusion: they should open it, for research purposes.

## The Missing Children

Children throughout the surrounding region have begun disappearing — not just Kenku, but Simiah, Katari, Humans, and others. Every victim has been a minor, and near every disappearance the same strange symbol is found.

Rumours naturally point toward Willy: he collects strange relics, he lives alone, people already think he's a necromancer, and he's been acting strangely. Somebody even swears they once saw children's bones outside his house — though by all other accounts, they were chicken bones.

Whatever is truly responsible remains unclear. The symbols connect the disappearances to the Sacred Spring, some suspect the Sea People are searching for children for reasons unknown, and others wonder whether the ancient tomb contains something capable of reaching beyond its prison. So far, the evidence points in all three directions at once.

That was the shape of it: a village quietly coming apart at half a dozen seams, all laid out over ale by five old friends who, for one more evening, were still just that — friends catching up, not yet an adventuring party.
