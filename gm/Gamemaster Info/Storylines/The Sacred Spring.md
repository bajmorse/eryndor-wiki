# The Sacred Spring

Connected to [[Full Session Notes]] in the player wiki.

Three independent facts are already established: it has anti-aging properties, a mass cult-like suicide once occurred there, and it is now rapidly drying up. Those demand answers.

## Current State

The spring lies deep in the forest below Sky-Bough. Its waters are reputed to slow aging — drinking from it occasionally seems to improve vitality, accelerate recovery, and subtly slow aging, though continuous consumption has consequences. It's the secret ingredient in [[Skybound Pale Ale]]. It has begun drying up in recent months.

## Established GM Truths

- The Sacred Spring is a separate physical location from the grove where the Ancient Pact's binding took place. The binding site holds the gateway to the Faewilds and the hidden pocket-realm garden/orchard the bound Fae tends; the spring lies downstream, fed by a river that runs through that grove. The Fae's garden-magic reaches the spring by way of that river, not because the Fae sits at the spring's own source. Sky-Bough's harvest is magically mirrored to the garden's condition through the same underlying mechanism — this isn't a separate phenomenon from [[The Ancient Pact]], just a different point where its effects surface.
- The spring itself, as a physical body of water, predates Sky-Bough's founding. Its magical/sacred qualities do not — those only began once the settlers bound the Fae in the grove upstream, so the anti-aging properties date to roughly the same era as Sky-Bough's founding, not before it.
- The Fae being the true source of the spring's magic is not known by anyone — not ordinary villagers, not Joe, and not even those within Sky-Bough who already know about the Ancient Pact and the bound Fae. This connection is a secret deeper than the Pact's own guarded knowledge.
- The anti-aging property and Skybound Pale Ale's distinctive taste/glow are both side effects of the water's proximity (via the river) to the Fae's garden-work. See [[Skybound Pale Ale]].
- The current drying is simply reduced overflow as the Fae's tending falters with the wards weakening — the water isn't going anywhere else.
- There is no connection between the old Drowning cult and the modern Testalot religion — nobody has ever suggested one. See [[Sir Testalot and the Testers]].
- Nobody formally controls access to the spring — it's simply deep in the forest, reachable by anyone willing to make the trek. Informally, the mayor's office cautions villagers against misuse or overuse of its waters. See [[The Mayor]].
- Overconsumption causes genuine addiction: the body grows reliant on the magical boost at a cellular level, producing real physical and mental symptoms — a faint fae-like glow beneath the skin along the veins, and mounting aggressive temperament tied to the craving. Withdrawal is severe: if access is cut off, the body ages unnaturally fast to compensate, and a long enough addiction results in death from that sudden aging. Addiction used to be a genuine problem among locals, but hasn't been for many years, thanks to better community support and greater vigilance in catching and cutting off addicts early.
- The old Drowning cult were not devout believers testing mortality — they were heavy, dependent drinkers of the spring's water, from an age before anyone understood the water's addictive properties. As their dependency deepened, they spent more and more time at the spring, and the sheer volume they consumed slowly forged an unintended mental connection between them and the bound Fae's own fractured, tormented mind — something none of them understood was happening.
- Their mass death wasn't a search for transcendence — it was them intentionally seeking an end to the mental anguish that the connection to the Fae's broken psyche caused. They meant to die. The legend's "willing sacrifice to transcend mortality" framing is a flattering distortion built up over generations of retelling; the real story is darker and sadder — uncontrolled addiction driving people to a breaking point that neither they nor anyone since has ever understood.
- The mass death, in the end, wasn't really "about" the spring's anti-aging properties — addiction was the mechanism, but the actual cause of death was psychic anguish from the unwitting Fae-connection, not any philosophy about eternal life.
- The lights witnesses reported moving beneath the water in the following nights were real — fragmented pieces of the Fae's own fractured consciousness, stirred loose and briefly surfacing through the connection at the moment its unwitting cultists died. Beyond that, nothing else supernatural happened; the aftermath was simply a grieving village trying to process a tragedy it didn't understand. Faint traces of that leftover Fae-consciousness may still linger in the spring to this day.
- The symbol found at each of [[The Missing Children]]'s disappearances has no connection to the spring or the old Drowning cult — a deliberate red herring; that mystery draws its answer from elsewhere.

## What the Party Knows

Everything under Current State, plus the old story of the Drowning. See [[Full Session Notes]].

## Rumours / What NPCs Believe

Decades ago, a religious community became obsessed with the spring, believing its ability to preserve life proved that death itself was a test — the faithful believed those who willingly surrendered their lives in the waters would transcend mortality rather than merely delay it. One night, dozens entered the spring and none returned; for several nights afterward, witnesses reported lights moving beneath the water. Centuries of retelling have reduced the event to "that time a cult drowned themselves in the spring." The drying spring is widely blamed on the failed [[The Ancient Pact|Ancient Pact]].
