# The Missing Children

Connected to [[Full Session Notes]] in the player wiki.

Deserves its own completely developed mystery independent of whether the players investigate it.

## Current State

Children throughout the surrounding region have begun disappearing — not just Kenku, but Simiah, Katari, Humans, and others. Every victim has been a minor, and near every disappearance the same strange symbol is found.

## Established GM Truths

- The disappearances and their symbol are not connected to [[The Sacred Spring]] or the old Drowning cult — deliberately ruled out.
- The disappearances have no connection to the ancient tomb or to [[The Sea People]] either — those remain unrelated threads, occurring at the same time purely by chance.
- The true culprit is an old, unnamed, wild fungal network — an ancient organism unrelated to any modern Fungril people or settlements. It predates modern Sky-Bough entirely.
- The network was dormant, incidentally suppressed for centuries by the bound Fae's ambient magic — the same field responsible for the good harvest and the forest's safety — though this was never a deliberate function of the Pact, just an unnoticed side effect. As the wards fail, the network is waking on its own coincidental timeline, not because the sacrifice failed. See [[The Ancient Pact]].
- The network has a single primary host: a real, physical creature the network has taken over and grown through. The host resides in tunnels threaded through the root systems that hold up the tree-borne parts of Sky-Bough — directly beneath the village. Sky-Bough's generations of growth simply built outward and downward right on top of where it lay sleeping, with nobody ever knowing it was there. This is a complete surprise to everyone, including long-time residents — no local tome, record, or history mentions anything beneath the roots.
- The network isn't scheming or planning an invasion. Its apparent "interest" in the village is really an ancient organism naturally reasserting awareness of a space it already considers its own, reaching into whatever nearby life it can reach — the same way roots or hyphae extend into anything reachable. There is no goal beyond instinct.
- Due to its current weakness, the network can only successfully implant on children. As it grows stronger, it will eventually be able to use adults too — this is a scaling threat, not a fixed limitation.
- Implanted children serve as living sensory extensions — nodes the network can perceive and faintly act through, letting it begin perceiving the world above after centuries of dormancy. Hosting a child also feeds and grows the network itself, especially crucial while it remains weak.
- The fog the network produces doesn't erase a child's physical presence — it erases recognition. People can still see the child fine, but nothing about them registers as *that specific person*; a parent looking directly at their own child sees an unfamiliar kid, not their son or daughter. This produces a real, felt disappearance — parents genuinely believe their child is gone — even though the child is, unknowingly, still walking around town as an unclaimed stranger.
- The children themselves don't understand what's happening to them. They're dimly aware something is wrong — parents and neighbours treating them like trespassers or unfamiliar loiterers — but don't land on the truth. It's confusing and distressing rather than a clean realization.
- Afflicted children are compelled to wander the most active, populated parts of the village — the market, the tavern, streets near homes — not to hide. This is what makes them useful as sensory extensions; it's instinctual reach on the network's part, not a deliberate strategy.
- The symbol found at each disappearance is the network's own natural mark — something about its growth pattern or fruiting structure — not a sigil deliberately left by an intelligence.
- The first child to disappear was a young Katari from one of Sky-Bough's ground-floor neighbourhoods — poorer districts at the base of the trees, not slums, but modest, and home more to ground-dwelling peoples like Katari and Humans than to the tree-dwelling Kenku and Simiah who live higher up. What mattered wasn't who this child was, but where they lived: proximity to wherever the host actually lies beneath the roots. Nothing about them personally marks them as special or deliberately chosen — but their home's location is a genuine, discoverable clue to the host's whereabouts, for anyone who thinks to connect it.
- The host remains physically contained beneath Sky-Bough, but its sensory reach is expanding outward as its strength returns — it has begun reaching into nearby farmsteads and smaller villages and crossings, extending its instinctual awareness to understand what lies around Sky-Bough, not just the village itself. This is the same undirected, instinctual expansion as everything else it does, just growing in range rather than only in what it can host.
- Transmission is spore-based and consistent: a child is implanted after close contact within the previous 12–24 hours with either an already-infected child, or the mycelium network itself where it's exposed (torn-up ground, roots, cellars, or other places beneath Sky-Bough where it reaches the surface). Once implanted, the spores take roughly 12–24 hours to germinate before the recognition-fog fully sets in — so the moment of exposure and the moment a child "disappears" don't line up cleanly, though the window itself is consistent enough to trace if someone digs into it.
- Once a child is implanted, their own body begins emitting the same spores as part of its growth — meaning affected children become vectors themselves, actively spreading the network's reach to whoever they're in close contact with next, not just passively hosting it.
- People genuinely have witnessed transmission happen — a child brushing against a strange patch of fungus, or simply standing close to another child who was already infected — they just never registered it as significant, since nothing about the moment looked alarming. During the 12–24 hour germination window there are minor physical signs (a small patch of oddly fibrous, faintly discoloured skin near the point of contact; the child seeming unusually quiet or distracted), but anyone who did notice these signs forgets having noticed them too, once the child's own fog fully sets in — the erasure works backward on memory, not just forward.
- The pattern connecting the disappearances isn't age or ancestry — it's contact. Every affected child had close contact with either an already-infected child or exposed mycelium within roughly 24 hours beforehand; that's the actual throughline someone would need to uncover.
- At least a dozen children have been affected so far, over the past few months, starting roughly a month after last year's failed sacrifice — consistent with how long the wards took to weaken enough elsewhere. See [[The Ancient Pact]].
- Most cases cluster in Sky-Bough's ground-floor districts, closest to the host, with a smaller number of more recent cases turning up in the outlying farmsteads and settlements as the network's reach has expanded.
- Victims skew toward the older edge of childhood, roughly ages 11–14 — old enough to be out and about unsupervised, which is also how they end up in contact with exposed mycelium or other infected children in the first place. Younger children aren't useful to the host as sensory extensions the same way, which is as much a factor as the network's own weakness in why it's children at all rather than adults.
- Victims are a genuine cross-section of Sky-Bough's communities, though skewed somewhat toward the ground-floor ancestries — fewer Kenku and Simiah relative to their larger populations in the village, more Katari, Humans, and others from the lower districts where the network is strongest. Fungril are entirely unaffected, though this is presently moot since no Fungril live in or near Sky-Bough.
- What makes Willy look guilty is entirely circumstantial and coincidental: decades of wandering have taken him deep into the forest and, more recently, into old cellars and root-hollows beneath the ground-floor districts — exactly the kind of places where the mycelium is exposed. He's genuinely, unknowingly been near several real contact sites, both before and after the fact, purely because that's where interesting old objects turn up. Combined with his sudden nightly appearances at the Bent Feather (read by everyone as a guilty conscience rather than someone building up the courage to confide in someone), his reputation as an odd hermit obsessed with strange objects, and his visible recognition of the symbol whenever it comes up, it adds up to a genuinely convincing — and completely wrong — circumstantial case. See [[Old Man Willy]].
- A second, false rumour blames witchcraft — dark rituals performed by travelling hedge-witches, particularly the nomadic Katari bands that pass near Sky-Bough. It has just enough coincidental basis to spread: a nomadic Katari band did pass close to Sky-Bough around when the disappearances began, and Katari are already overrepresented among both the missing and the ground-floor population generally. It's entirely baseless — nothing about any travelling group is connected to the truth. [[Nyx Ashwisk]], as a Katari hedge witch who visits Sky-Bough regularly, finds herself an object of exactly this suspicion despite having nothing to do with any of it.
- Long before Sky-Bough existed — well before the Unification — an ancient, now-entirely-extinct culture lived in genuine, willing symbiosis with the Mycelium Host, revering it as an almost-deific spirit of the land. A select few, chosen as a mark of high honor, willingly became hosts. During germination they would travel to a specific ceremonial site, and once it completed, the resulting mark was carved or imprinted onto a ceremonial stone or piece of wood to commemorate them.
- Unlike today, hosting caused no "forgetting." That erasure is a purely modern side effect of the network reaching out blind and uncontrolled, without the old rites and rapport this culture once provided. Back then, a community could freely speak with a host — and speaking to one was like speaking to all of them, instant shared senses across the whole network. Hosting also granted minor elemental control over earth and water to those chosen.
- This culture died out through no war, disaster, or malice — just the slow, ironic cost of a practice they loved completely. Generation after generation, their most capable people were the ones honored with hosting, meaning the community kept giving up some of its best chances to sustain itself. Combined with being small and isolated to begin with, that attrition alone was enough to end them over centuries. They didn't assimilate into any other people — they simply dwindled to nothing, leaving no descendants, no oral histories, no surviving records among any currently-known people.
- A handful of faint traces still exist for anyone willing to dig for them. Somewhere in the forest, partial ruins — old foundations, worn ceremonial markers — lie buried beneath rock and root, reclaimed almost entirely by the land and unrecognizable as anything but odd old stonework unless someone already suspects what they're looking for. Separately, Fungril oral tradition preserves a vague, half-forgotten myth about an ancient people who could "speak through root and spore" — treated by modern Fungril themselves as pure folklore, with no genealogical link or real detail, but eerily consistent with the truth for anyone who dug deep enough to notice the resemblance.
- Once its willing hosts were gone, the network — deprived of what fed and connected it — went naturally dormant on its own, quiet enough that it never revealed itself even when Sky-Bough's earliest settlers first arrived, years or decades before the Ancient Pact was struck. When the settlers later bound the Fae for entirely unrelated reasons, its ambient magic incidentally deepened and extended that already-existing dormancy for centuries more. The wards failing now lifts both layers at once — and the network has been quietly regaining strength throughout that whole span, not just recently.

## Rumours / What NPCs Believe

Rumours naturally point toward [[Old Man Willy]]: he collects strange relics, he lives alone, people already think he's a necromancer, and he's been acting strangely. Somebody even swears they once saw children's bones outside his house — most people assume they were chicken bones, but nobody's actually checked closely enough to be sure.

A second strain of rumour blames witchcraft — dark rituals performed by travelling hedge-witches, especially the nomadic Katari bands that pass near Sky-Bough. It's baseless, but circulates widely enough that any visiting hedge witch, [[Nyx Ashwisk]] included, might feel the weight of it.
